Sahib et-Tabaa Mosque

Tunis · 1808-1814 · Husainids

Directions See on the map

Overview

Overlooking Halfaouine Square, north of the medina of Tunis, this early 19th-century mosque is the last great mosque built in Tunis before the French protectorate. Its columns and walls of Italian marble make it the Tunisian oratory most marked by European influence.

Photos

History

It bears the name of Youssef Sahib et-Tabaa, born around 1765 in Moldavia, enslaved as a child and taken into the service of Prince Hammouda Pacha in 1781; he became his keeper of the seal (sahib et-tabaa) in 1782, then his chief minister. He had the mosque built from 1808 to 1814 under the master builder Hadj Sassi ibn Frija, and inaugurated it in 1814. Wounded in an attack at the Bardo in September 1814, he died in January 1815: the minaret remained unfinished until the 1970s.

Architecture

Built above shops and warehouses, on a base of dressed stone taken from the ruins of Carthage, the mosque towers over the square. An elbowed staircase climbs to the east gallery, with its double colonnade and five arches open onto the square; the octagonal minaret stands at its end. Three galleries frame the prayer hall, which has 9 naves and 7 bays and is covered with barrel vaults. The two end bays, along the qibla wall and the opposite wall, each carry three domes: one in the centre and one at each corner.

In detail

According to the Discover Islamic Art virtual museum entry, no other Tunisian oratory shows such a clear Western influence. The marble, more abundant than elsewhere, came directly from Carrara in Italy. The slender fluted columns carry composite capitals, an Italian interpretation of the Tunisian model of the Turkish period; the arches, barely horseshoe-shaped, depart from the classic horseshoe, and the lintels bear neo-Baroque reliefs. Ceramic tiles are left only to the upper walls. The mihrab and the fixed minbar, in polychrome marble, mix pseudo-Gothic arcading, flowers and touches of gilding. The east gallery has an outdoor mihrab for prayer in hot weather.

Role and influence

The mosque was the heart of a large complex founded by the minister to serve the Halfaouine quarter: two madrasas, a souk, a hammam, a public fountain (sabil), a fondouk, his palace and his tomb, which, like the madrasas, is entered from the north façade.

Visiting

It is first of all a place of prayer: dress modestly, take off your shoes to enter the prayer hall and avoid prayer times. In several great mosques, non-Muslim visitors may enter the courtyard but not the prayer hall. Opening times change: ask on site.
