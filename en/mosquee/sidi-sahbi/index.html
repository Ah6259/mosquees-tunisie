Zawiya of Sidi Sahbi (Mosque of the Barber)

Kairouan · 17th century · Muradids

Directions See on the map

Overview

The mausoleum of Abu Zama'a al-Balawi, a Companion of the Prophet ﷺ, whom the people of Kairouan call 'Sidi Sahbi': the patron saint of Kairouan. Its courtyards covered with ceramic tiles and carved plaster make it one of the city's most beautiful monuments.

Photos

History

Abu Zama'a al-Balawi died in battle in 654 (year 34 of the Hijra). He is said to have carried a few hairs of the Prophet's ﷺ beard, hence the name 'Mosque of the Barber'. The old building was entirely renovated in the 17th century by the Muradid beys: Hammouda Pacha rebuilt the zawiya, the madrasa and the minaret around 1662, then Mohamed Bey rebuilt the dome of the mausoleum around 1681-1685.

Architecture

A vast complex outside the walls of the medina: an entrance courtyard and an Andalusian-style minaret, a colonnaded patio, a domed hall, then a courtyard whose walls are covered with polychrome tiles and finely carved plaster, leading to the burial chamber. A madrasa, with its students' rooms and prayer hall, completes the complex.

Role and influence

It is one of the places most visited by Tunisians, especially for the Mawlid festival; marriage contracts and circumcisions are also celebrated there. In the past, caravans from the Maghreb gathered there before setting off on pilgrimage to Mecca.

Stories and accounts

TraditionThe companion who kept hairs from the Prophet's beard ﷺ

Abu Zam'a al-Balawi, a companion of the Prophet ﷺ, died fighting the Byzantines around 654 and was buried here, even before Kairouan was founded. According to tradition, he carried a few hairs from the Prophet's beard ﷺ. This is where the nickname “Mosque of the Barber” comes from: he was not a barber. “Sidi Sahbi” means “my lord the Companion”. Most of the present building dates from the 17th century.

Source: Tunisie Patrimoine · Wikipedia

Historical factTwo beys and tiles from Tunis

In the 14th century a first mausoleum was built over the tomb of Abu Zam'a al-Balawi. In 1629 Hammouda Pacha Bey, founder of the Hammouda Pacha mosque in Tunis, greatly enlarged it with a prayer hall and rooms for pilgrims. His successor Mohamed Bey rebuilt the mausoleum's dome between 1681 and 1685, then added a minaret and a madrasa. The walls are covered with painted tiles from Qallaline, the potters' quarter of Tunis, in blue, green and yellow, topped by carved stucco.

Source: Wikipedia

TraditionThe Mawlid and the little boys

Kairouan celebrates the feast of the Prophet's birth ﷺ (the Mawlid) with special fervour. In the past, after his circumcision, a little boy would cross the town with his family and visit the mausoleum of Sidi Sahbi. Even today, during the Mawlid, families and associations organise circumcisions there for poor or orphaned children, amid ululations, incense and henna. After the feast, Kairouan families come to the mausoleum to recite the Fatiha.

Source: La Presse (2025) · Vikidia

Visiting

It is first of all a place of prayer: dress modestly, take off your shoes to enter the prayer hall and avoid prayer times. In several great mosques, non-Muslim visitors may enter the courtyard but not the prayer hall. Opening times change: ask on site.
