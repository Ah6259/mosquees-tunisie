Al-Zaytuna Mosque

Tunis · 698 · rebuilt in 864

Directions See on the map

Overview

The great mosque of the Tunis medina, the oldest and largest in the capital. For more than a thousand years it was also a great Islamic university.

Photos

History

Tradition says the prayer place was founded in 698 by Hassan ibn al-Nu'man after the taking of Carthage; other sources credit the Umayyad governor Ubayd Allah ibn al-Habhab, around 732. Almost nothing remains of that first building: the mosque was entirely rebuilt in 864 by the Aghlabid emir Abu Ibrahim Ahmad. Each dynasty then embellished it: the 'bahou' dome under the Zirids (around 990), cisterns and doors under the Hafsids, the eastern gallery in 1637. Its present Almohad-style minaret dates from 1894.

Architecture

As in Kairouan and Córdoba, a courtyard surrounded by galleries leads to a 'hypostyle' prayer hall: about 160 ancient columns, many brought from Carthage, form 15 aisles. The wider central aisle leads to the mihrab, crowned by a 9th-century dome. The bahou dome at the entrance of the hall alternates ochre stone and red brick. The 1894 minaret repeats the lozenge pattern of the Kasbah Mosque minaret.

Role and influence

The Zitouna was one of the oldest centres of learning in the Muslim world: each scholar leaned against a column, his students sitting in a circle around him. It trained great Maliki jurists such as Ibn Arafa, a contemporary of Ibn Khaldun. In 1958 teaching left the mosque; it continues today at Ez-Zitouna University. It is said that prayer has never been interrupted there.

Visiting

It is first of all a place of prayer: dress modestly, take off your shoes to enter the prayer hall and avoid prayer times. In several great mosques, non-Muslim visitors may enter the courtyard but not the prayer hall. Opening times change: ask on site.
