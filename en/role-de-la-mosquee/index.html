The role of the mosque in Islam, from the time of the Prophet ﷺ to today

The mosque has never been only a place of prayer. Since the first mosque in Medina, it has also been a school, a place for counsel, welcome and mutual help. Here is how this role has changed over time.

The stages

  1. 622-632 In the time of the Prophet ﷺ: the mosque, heart of Medina
  2. 632-750 The caliphs: each town grows around its great mosque
  3. 9th-13th century Mosque-universities and libraries
  4. 12th-18th century The great complexes: mosque, school, hospital, kitchen for the poor
  5. 19th-20th century The Quranic school and the great scholars
  6. Today Today: an open place, serving everyone

622-632

In the time of the Prophet ﷺ: the mosque, heart of Medina

On arriving in Medina in 622, the Prophet ﷺ first had a mosque built: at Quba, then in the centre of the town. He worked on it himself and carried bricks with his companions. The building was simple: mud-brick walls, palm trunks as columns, a roof of palm leaves.

In this mosque, the life of the whole community came together:

  • prayer, five times a day, and the Friday sermon;
  • teaching: the Quran and the religion were learned there, in circles around the Prophet ﷺ;
  • shelter: poor companions without family, the “people of the Suffa”, lived under a shelter of the mosque;
  • counsel and decisions: the Prophet ﷺ consulted his companions there and received the delegations of tribes and neighbouring lands;
  • mutual help: alms were brought there and shared among those in need;
  • care: after the Battle of the Trench (627), a tent was set up in the mosque to treat the wounded Sa'd ibn Mu'adh (reported by al-Bukhari); according to the biographer Ibn Ishaq, it was the tent of Rufayda, a woman who cared for the wounded.

632-750

The caliphs: each town grows around its great mosque

New towns were built around their great mosque: Kufa and Basra in Iraq, Fustat in Egypt (Mosque of Amr ibn al-As, 641-642), Kairouan in Tunisia (670). The governor gave the Friday sermon there, the judge (qadi) delivered justice there, and news and decisions were announced to everyone.

Under the Umayyads, great mosques such as that of Damascus (705-715) also displayed the beauty and power of the new state: mosaics, a vast courtyard, minarets.

9th-13th century

Mosque-universities and libraries

Study circles (halaqa) around a scholar became real schools. Fatima al-Fihriya founded the al-Qarawiyyin mosque in Fez in 859; al-Azhar opened in Cairo in 970-972; in Tunis, the Zitouna trained judges, scholars and men of letters for centuries, and in Kairouan the Great Mosque was a major centre of Maliki law.

The Quran, hadith and law were taught there, but also the Arabic language, grammar, arithmetic and astronomy, useful for prayer times and the direction of Mecca. Mosques had their libraries, enriched by gifts of books made as waqf (property given for ever to the common good).

12th-18th century

The great complexes: mosque, school, hospital, kitchen for the poor

Around the mosque, pious foundations (waqf) funded schools (madrasas), hospitals (bimaristans), fountains, baths and kitchens for the poor. In Istanbul, the Süleymaniye complex (1550-1557, architect Sinan) brought together the mosque, several schools, a hospital, baths, a caravanserai and a public kitchen (imaret) that fed students, travellers and the poor every day.

In Tunisia too, mosques, madrasas and zawiyas welcomed students and travellers; the great mosques of Tunis are surrounded by souks, libraries and foundations that keep them alive.

19th-20th century

The Quranic school and the great scholars

In every neighbourhood, the Quranic school (kuttab), often near the mosque, taught children to read, write and recite the Quran on wooden boards. The great mosques remained places of learning: at the Zitouna, for example, Mohamed Tahar Ben Achour (1879-1973) taught; he wrote a major commentary on the Quran.

Today

Today: an open place, serving everyone

The mosque remains first of all a place of communal prayer: the five prayers, the Friday sermon, the prayers of the two Eids and, during Ramadan, the night prayers (tarawih). Many mosques also offer:

  • Quran and Arabic lessons for children and adults;
  • fast-breaking meals during Ramadan, open to everyone;
  • the collection of the end-of-Ramadan alms (zakat al-fitr) and help for families in need;
  • a welcome for visitors who want to discover Islam and architecture: the great mosques are also monuments.
