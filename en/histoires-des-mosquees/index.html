The mosques of the Prophet's time ﷺ

The Prophet's Mosque ﷺ (Medina)

Account with a weak chainTamim ad-Dari and the first lamps

At first, the mosque of Medina was lit at night by burning palm leaves. Tamim ad-Dari, a companion who came from Syria, brought lamps, oil and ropes; one Friday evening they were hung up and he lit them. The Prophet ﷺ found the mosque shining with light and asked who had done it. He was told: “Tamim”. He said: “You have illuminated Islam; may Allah illuminate you in this world and the next.” This account is reported by the books of biographies of the companions (Ibn Hajar, al-Isaba) with a weak chain; another text, “the first to light lamps in the mosques was Tamim ad-Dari” (Ibn Majah no. 760), is very weak.

Source: Ibn Hajar, al-Isaba (récit) ; Ibn Majah 760

Authentic hadithThe palm trunk that wept

The Prophet ﷺ used to give the Friday sermon standing, leaning against a palm trunk. When a minbar was made for him and he went up on it, the trunk began to cry out like a child. The Prophet ﷺ came down and held it close, and the trunk slowly calmed down. He explained that it was weeping for the remembrance of Allah it used to hear.

Source: al-Bukhari 3584

Authentic hadithThe people of the Suffa and the bowl of milk

At the back of the mosque, a shelter, the Suffa, housed poor companions without family or home; Abu Hurayra was one of them. One day when he was very hungry, the Prophet ﷺ asked him to call all the people of the Suffa to share a single bowl of milk. Abu Hurayra feared there would be nothing left for him. They all drank until they were full, then he did, then the Prophet ﷺ.

Source: al-Bukhari 6452

Authentic hadithThe two orphans' land and the bricks

In Medina, the Prophet's she-camel knelt on a plot where dates were dried. It belonged to two orphans, Sahl and Suhayl, who wanted to give it; the Prophet ﷺ refused the gift and bought it from them. Then he carried the bricks himself with his companions to build the mosque.

Source: al-Bukhari 3906

Authentic hadithThe first call to prayer

The Muslims were looking for a way to call people to prayer: a bell? a horn? Abdallah ibn Zayd saw in a dream a man who taught him the words of the call (the adhan). The Prophet ﷺ said it was a true vision and asked him to teach them to Bilal, who had a stronger voice. Umar came and said he had had the same dream.

Source: Abu Dawud 499

Authentic hadithA garden of Paradise: the Rawda

The Prophet ﷺ said: "Between my house and my pulpit lies a garden among the gardens of Paradise, and my pulpit is upon my fountain." This small space, between the room where he lived and the pulpit from which he preached, is called the Rawda today. It is recognisable by its green carpets, unlike the red carpets of the rest of the mosque. Visitors often wait a long time to pray there for a few moments. Authentic hadith reported by al-Bukhari and Muslim.

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 1196 · Sahih Muslim 1391

Authentic hadithOne prayer worth a thousand

The Prophet ﷺ said that one prayer in his mosque is better than a thousand prayers in any other mosque, except the Sacred Mosque in Mecca. He also said that one should set out on a journey only to three mosques: the Sacred Mosque, his mosque in Medina and al-Aqsa Mosque in Jerusalem. This is why so many pilgrims pass through Medina before or after Mecca. Both hadiths are authentic (al-Bukhari).

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 1190 · Sahih al-Bukhari 1189

Authentic hadithThe Bedouin and the Prophet's gentleness ﷺ

One day a Bedouin from the desert began to urinate in a corner of the mosque. People rose to rebuke him, but the Prophet ﷺ told them to leave him alone. He then had a bucket of water poured over the spot. And he told his companions: "You were sent to make things easy, not to make them difficult." According to other versions, he then gently explained to the man that mosques are for prayer and the remembrance of God. Authentic hadith (al-Bukhari, Muslim).

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 220 · Sahih Muslim 285

Authentic hadithThe one who swept the mosque

A black person, a woman according to most versions, used to sweep the Prophet's mosque ﷺ every day. She died and was buried at night without the Prophet ﷺ being told, because people thought her unimportant. When he noticed her absence and learned of her death, he asked: "Why did you not inform me?" He asked to be shown her grave and prayed the funeral prayer over it. The story shows that no service to the mosque is small. Authentic hadith (al-Bukhari, Muslim).

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 458 · Sahih Muslim 956

Authentic hadithThe Abyssinians' spear display on a feast day

On a feast day, some Abyssinians were giving a display of skill with their spears in the mosque. Aisha, the Prophet's wife ﷺ, wanted to watch. The Prophet ﷺ stood at the door of her room and screened her with his cloak so that she could see. She watched as long as she wished, until she herself grew tired of it. The story shows that the mosque was also a place of life and joy. Authentic hadith (al-Bukhari, Muslim).

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 454 · Sahih Muslim 892

Authentic hadithThumama, the prisoner tied to a pillar

Thumama ibn Uthal, chief of the Banu Hanifa tribe, was captured and tied to one of the pillars of the mosque. For three days he saw at close range the life of the Muslims, their prayers and the way they treated one another. On the third day, the Prophet ﷺ ordered him to be released without asking anything in return. Thumama went to wash, then came back of his own accord and embraced Islam. He said the face he had hated most had become the one he loved most. Authentic hadith (al-Bukhari, Muslim).

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 4372 · Sahih Muslim 1764

Authentic hadithKa'b ibn Malik: fifty days of silence

Ka'b ibn Malik stayed behind from the Tabuk expedition without any real excuse. On the Prophet's ﷺ return he refused to lie and admitted his fault in the mosque. For fifty days nobody spoke to him; he came to pray in the mosque and greeted the Prophet ﷺ without knowing whether he answered. Then God's forgiveness was announced. Ka'b hurried to the mosque, where Talha rose to congratulate him, and the Prophet's ﷺ face shone with joy. Authentic hadith (al-Bukhari, Muslim); the Quran mentions these three men in Surah 9 (at-Tawba), verse 118.

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 4418 · Sahih Muslim 2769

Authentic hadithThe first pulpit, made of tamarisk wood

At first the Prophet ﷺ preached standing, leaning against a palm trunk. A woman of the Ansar offered to have her slave, a carpenter, make him a pulpit. It was made of tamarisk wood from the forest of al-Ghaba near Medina. The Prophet ﷺ prayed and preached on it, saying: "I did this so that you may follow me and learn my prayer." According to historical accounts, it had only three steps. Authentic hadith for its making (al-Bukhari, Muslim); the three steps come from history books.

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 917 · Sahih Muslim 544

Authentic hadithThe she-camel that chose the spot

When the Prophet ﷺ arrived in Medina after the Hijra, every family wanted to host him. He let his she-camel walk, and she knelt down on a plot used for drying dates. That is where the mosque was built. The Prophet ﷺ first stayed nearby with Abu Ayyub al-Ansari while the mosque and its rooms were built. The kneeling of the camel is authentic (al-Bukhari); the often-quoted words "leave her, she is guided" come from the sira books.

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 3906 · Wikipedia (Al-Masjid an-Nabawi)

Authentic hadithUmar asks to rest beside his two companions

The Prophet ﷺ was buried in Aisha's room, adjoining the mosque, and then Abu Bakr beside him. When the caliph Umar was stabbed, he sent his son to ask Aisha's permission to be buried next to them. Aisha replied that she had kept that place for herself, but she would give it to him. Umar asked that the request be made again after his death, in case she changed her mind; she agreed again. That is why the three graves are together today, under the green dome. Authentic hadith (al-Bukhari).

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 1392 · Sahih al-Bukhari 3700

Authentic hadithFrom palm trunks to stone: Umar, then Uthman

Abdullah ibn Umar described the first mosque: walls of sun-dried mud bricks, a roof of palm leaves and pillars of palm trunks. Abu Bakr changed nothing. Umar enlarged it using the same materials. Uthman enlarged it greatly and rebuilt it with carved stone, plaster and a roof of teak wood. This testimony is an authentic hadith (al-Bukhari 446); historians date Umar's enlargement to about 638 and Uthman's to about 649-650.

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 446 · Wikipedia (Al-Masjid an-Nabawi)

Historical factAl-Walid and the first mihrab niche

Around 707-711, the Umayyad caliph al-Walid I had the mosque rebuilt and enlarged. His governor in Medina, Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz, directed the works. The rooms of the Prophet's ﷺ wives were then brought inside the mosque, together with the tomb. Minarets were built and, according to historians, the first concave mihrab niche set into the wall, a model later copied all over the world. Historical fact recorded by the chroniclers of Medina.

Source: Wikipedia (Al-Masjid an-Nabawi) · Britannica (Mosque of the Prophet)

Historical factTwo fires: 1256 and 1481

In 1256 (654 AH), an accidental fire destroyed much of the mosque, starting, according to the chronicles, from a servant's lamp. The Mamluk sultans of Egypt rebuilt it. In 1481, lightning struck the main minaret and caused a second great fire. The Mamluk sultan Qaitbay then had the mosque and the dome over the tomb rebuilt. Historical facts recorded notably by the Medina historian al-Samhudi, who witnessed the second fire.

Source: Wikipedia (Al-Masjid an-Nabawi) · Wikipedia (al-Samhudi)

Historical factThe Green Dome, painted in 1837

For centuries there was no dome above the Prophet's ﷺ tomb. A first wooden dome was built in 1279 under the Mamluk sultan Qalawun; it was rebuilt several times and changed colour, white then blue. The Ottoman sultan Mahmud II had it rebuilt in 1818, and it was painted green for the first time in 1837. Since then it has been called "the Green Dome", the best-known image of Medina. Historical fact.

Source: Wikipedia (Green Dome)

Historical factFrom the Ottomans to giant umbrellas

In the 19th century, the Ottoman sultan Abdülmecid I had almost the whole mosque rebuilt (about 1849-1861), with its small domes and calligraphy, part of which can still be seen today. In the 20th century, the kings of Saudi Arabia enlarged it several times; King Fahd's great extension (1984-1994) multiplied its area. In the 2010s, 250 giant umbrellas that open and close automatically were installed on the courtyards to shade worshippers from the sun. Historical facts.

Source: Wikipedia (Al-Masjid an-Nabawi) · Britannica (Mosque of the Prophet)

Account with a weak chainThe Christians of Najran pray in the mosque

Around year 9 or 10 of the Hijra, a delegation of Christians from Najran, in southern Arabia, came to Medina to talk with the Prophet ﷺ. According to Ibn Ishaq, when the time of their prayer came, they wished to pray in the mosque; some people wanted to stop them, but the Prophet ﷺ said: "Leave them." They then prayed facing east. The delegation's visit is well known, but this detail about the prayer comes from a report with a broken chain, so it is weak. It is nevertheless often cited as an example of hospitality.

Source: Wikipedia (Event of Mubahala)

Account with a weak chainAbu Lubaba and the Pillar of Repentance

According to the sira books, the companion Abu Lubaba regretted letting a secret slip during the siege of the Banu Qurayza. He tied himself to a pillar of the mosque and swore not to untie himself until God forgave him. He stayed there several days, untied only for prayer, until his forgiveness was announced; the Prophet ﷺ himself untied him. This pillar, in the Rawda, still bears the name "Pillar of Abu Lubaba" or "Pillar of Repentance". The story comes from Ibn Ishaq and others; scholars discuss the strength of its chains.

Source: Wikipedia (Abu Lubaba ibn Abd al-Mundhir)

The Quba Mosque (Medina)

QuranThe first mosque, founded on piety

Quba is the first mosque built by the Prophet ﷺ, when he arrived near Medina. The Quran mentions it as the mosque founded on piety from the first day (sura 9, verse 108). The Prophet ﷺ went there every Saturday, and he said: whoever purifies himself at home and then comes to pray there has the reward of an 'umra (Ibn Majah no. 1412, authentic according to al-Albani).

Source: Coran 9:108 · Ibn Majah 1412

Authentic hadithEvery Saturday, on foot or riding

Abdullah ibn Umar relates that the Prophet ﷺ used to go to the Quba mosque every Saturday, sometimes on foot and sometimes riding, and pray two units of prayer there. Ibn Umar himself kept doing so all his life. Even today, many residents and visitors of Medina go to pray at Quba on Saturdays. Authentic hadith (al-Bukhari, Muslim).

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 1193 · Sahih Muslim 1399

Authentic hadithMen who love to purify themselves

The Quran, in Surah 9 (at-Tawba), verse 108, speaks of men in this mosque who love to purify themselves, and says that God loves those who purify themselves. According to a hadith reported by Abu Hurayra, the verse concerns the people of Quba, who used to wash with water after relieving themselves. So a simple habit of cleanliness became praise in the Quran. Hadith reported by Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi, graded authentic by al-Albani.

Source: Sunan Abi Dawud 44 · Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3100

Authentic hadithThe first days of the Hijra

After leaving Mecca with Abu Bakr, the Prophet ﷺ first arrived at Quba, a village at the southern edge of Medina. The people of Medina went out every morning to wait for him in the heat. He stayed with the Banu Amr ibn Awf tribe a little more than ten nights (fourteen according to Anas) and founded the mosque there. Then he went on to the centre of Medina, where he built his own mosque. Authentic hadith (al-Bukhari).

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 3906 · Sahih al-Bukhari 3932

Authentic hadithThe news reaches Quba at dawn

The day after the change of qibla, the people of Quba were praying the dawn prayer, still facing Jerusalem. A man came and told them that the Prophet ﷺ had been ordered to pray towards the Kaaba. Without interrupting their prayer, they turned towards Mecca. The story shows the trust of the first Muslims in news brought by a single reliable man. Authentic hadith (al-Bukhari, Muslim).

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 403 · Sahih Muslim 526

Historical factToday's Quba, the work of an Egyptian architect

The Quba mosque was restored and rebuilt many times over the centuries, notably by the Ottomans. The present all-white building, with its six main domes and four minarets, was designed by the Egyptian architect Abdel-Wahed El-Wakil and completed around 1986. It draws on traditional forms of Arab architecture. Historical fact.

Source: Wikipedia (Quba Mosque) · Wikipedia (Abdel-Wahed El-Wakil)

The Mosque of the Two Qiblas (Medina)

Authentic hadithTurning towards Mecca in the middle of a prayer

For sixteen or seventeen months, the Muslims of Medina prayed towards Jerusalem; then the Quran ordered them to turn towards the Kaaba (sura 2, verse 144). A man passed by people in the middle of their prayer and told them: they turned at once towards Mecca without breaking off their prayer (al-Bukhari no. 40). According to the tradition of Medina, this took place in the mosque of the Banu Salima, called ever since the “Mosque of the Two Qiblas”.

Source: Coran 2:144 · al-Bukhari 40

QuranThe face turned towards the sky

During the first months in Medina, Muslims prayed towards Jerusalem, but the Prophet ﷺ hoped to pray towards the Kaaba, the house built by Abraham. The Quran, in Surah 2 (al-Baqara), verse 144, describes God seeing his face turning towards the sky, and commands him to turn his face towards the Sacred Mosque wherever he is. Verses 142 to 150 of the same surah deal with this change of qibla and the questions it raised.

Source: Coran 2:144 (Quran.com) · Sahih al-Bukhari 40

Authentic hadithSixteen or seventeen months towards Jerusalem

The companion al-Bara' ibn Azib relates that after arriving in Medina, the Prophet ﷺ prayed towards Jerusalem for sixteen or seventeen months. After the change, some wondered what would become of the prayers of Muslims who had died before that day. The answer came in the Quran, Surah 2 (al-Baqara), verse 143, which assures that God does not let their faith be lost. Authentic hadith (al-Bukhari, Muslim).

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 40 · Sahih Muslim 525

TraditionWas the Prophet ﷺ himself praying here?

The mosque was first called the mosque of the Banu Salima, a tribe of the Ansar. According to a tradition reported by Ibn Sa'd, the Prophet ﷺ was visiting Umm Bishr there and leading the noon prayer when the order to face the Kaaba came: he is said to have prayed half the prayer towards Jerusalem and the other half towards Mecca. The authentic reports in Bukhari say instead that a companion brought the news to people at prayer. Both versions explain the name "mosque of the two qiblas".

Source: Wikipedia (Masjid al-Qiblatayn) · Sahih al-Bukhari 4486

Historical factTwo mihrabs, then only one

For centuries the mosque kept the memory of its two directions: a mihrab towards Mecca and an old mihrab towards Jerusalem, to the north. The Ottoman sultan Suleiman the Magnificent had it restored around 1543. When it was rebuilt in 1987 under King Fahd, the old mihrab facing Jerusalem was removed to avoid any confusion among worshippers; only one remains. Historical fact.

Source: Wikipedia (Masjid al-Qiblatayn)

The Sacred Mosque and the Kaaba (Mecca)

Historical factThe Black Stone and the “Trustworthy one”

Before the revelation, when the Prophet ﷺ was about 35, the tribe of Quraysh rebuilt the Kaaba. The clans quarrelled over the honour of putting the Black Stone back, to the point of risking war. They agreed that the first person to enter the mosque would decide: it was Muhammad ﷺ, whom everyone called “al-Amin”, the Trustworthy. He placed the stone on a cloak, had each clan hold one edge, then set it in place himself.

Source: Ibn Hicham, Sira ; Musnad Ahmad 15504

QuranAbraham and Ishmael raise the Kaaba

The Quran tells that the prophet Abraham and his son Ishmael raised the foundations of the House in Mecca together (sura 2, verse 127). While building, they prayed to God to accept their work. The Quran also says that God showed Abraham the site of the House and told him to keep it pure for those who pray there (sura 22, verse 26). For Muslims, the Kaaba is therefore above all a house dedicated to the One God.

Source: Coran 2:127 (quran.com) · Coran 22:26 (quran.com)

Authentic hadithHagar, Ishmael and the spring of Zamzam

In a long account by Ibn Abbas recorded by al-Bukhari, Abraham left his wife Hagar and their baby Ishmael in the empty valley of Mecca with a little water and some dates. When the water ran out, Hagar ran seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwa looking for help. An angel then made water gush out near the child: this is the spring of Zamzam. Nomads of the Jurhum tribe settled near the water, and the town began. The pilgrims' walk between Safa and Marwa recalls Hagar's search.

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 3364 · Wikipédia : Zamzam

QuranThe Station of Abraham

Near the Kaaba stands the Station of Abraham (Maqam Ibrahim), a stone that, according to tradition, bears Abraham's footprints, on which he stood while building. The Quran asks believers to take the Station of Abraham as a place of prayer (sura 2, verse 125) and names it among the clear signs of the House (sura 3, verse 97). According to al-Bukhari, Umar ibn al-Khattab had wished people would pray there, and the verse came down in agreement. Today the stone is kept inside a small case of glass and gilded metal.

Source: Coran 2:125 (quran.com) · Sahih al-Bukhari 402

QuranAbraham's call to pilgrimage

The Quran tells that God commanded Abraham to call people to the pilgrimage, and that they would come on foot and on lean mounts from every distant path (sura 22, verse 27). Commentators add that Abraham asked how his voice could reach so far, and was told to call while God would carry the call; this detail comes from the commentaries, not from the Quranic text. Muslims see the millions of pilgrims who come each year from all over the world as the answer to that call.

Source: Coran 22:27 (quran.com) · Tafsir Ibn Kathir 22:27 (quran.com)

Authentic hadithThe first mosque on earth

The companion Abu Dharr once asked the Prophet ﷺ which mosque was built first on earth. He answered: the Sacred Mosque in Mecca, then al-Aqsa Mosque in Jerusalem, forty years later. This report is recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim. The Quran also says that the first House set up for mankind is the one at Bakka, another name for Mecca (sura 3, verse 96).

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 3366 · Coran 3:96 (quran.com)

QuranThe Year of the Elephant

Around 570, Abraha, governor of Yemen for the kingdom of Abyssinia, marched on Mecca with an army and at least one elephant to destroy the Kaaba. Abd al-Muttalib, the Prophet's grandfather, is said to have asked only for his camels, saying the House had a Lord to protect it (an account from the Sira books). The Quran tells that God sent flocks of birds against the army, pelting it with stones, and it was destroyed (sura 105, The Elephant). According to tradition, the Prophet ﷺ was born that same year.

Source: Coran, sourate 105 (quran.com) · Wikipedia : Year of the Elephant

Authentic hadithThe Hijr of Ishmael

Beside the Kaaba, a low semicircular wall surrounds a space called the Hijr of Ishmael, or Hatim. Aisha reported that the Prophet ﷺ told her this space was part of the House, but that Quraysh, when rebuilding the Kaaba, ran short of lawful money and left it outside. He added that, had her people not so recently left paganism, he would have rebuilt the Kaaba on Abraham's foundations (al-Bukhari and Muslim). That is why pilgrims walk around the Hijr, and praying inside it counts as praying inside the Kaaba.

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 1586 · Wikipédia : Hijr Ismaïl

QuranThe direction of prayer turned to the Kaaba

After the emigration to Medina, Muslims prayed facing Jerusalem for sixteen or seventeen months, as al-Bara' ibn Azib reports in al-Bukhari. The Prophet ﷺ longed to face the Kaaba and often turned his face toward the sky. The Quran tells that God then commanded him to turn his face toward the Sacred Mosque (sura 2, verse 144). Since then, Muslims all over the world pray facing the Kaaba, called the qibla.

Source: Coran 2:144 (quran.com) · Sahih al-Bukhari 399

Authentic hadithThe conquest of Mecca and the broken idols

In year 8 of the Hijra (630), the Prophet ﷺ entered Mecca almost without fighting. According to Abdullah ibn Mas'ud, reported by al-Bukhari, there were three hundred and sixty idols around the Kaaba. He struck them with a stick in his hand while reciting the verse saying that truth has come and falsehood has vanished (sura 17, verse 81). The images inside the Kaaba were also removed, and the House was returned to the worship of the One God.

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 4287 · Coran 17:81 (quran.com)

Authentic hadithThe Prophet prays inside the Kaaba

On the day of the conquest, the Prophet ﷺ entered the Kaaba with Usama ibn Zayd, Bilal and Uthman ibn Talha, the keeper of the key, and the door was closed. Abdullah ibn Umar, the first to reach them as they came out, asked Bilal where the Prophet had prayed. Bilal showed him the spot, between two pillars, facing the door. This is reported by al-Bukhari and Muslim; Ibn Umar later regretted not asking how many prayer units he had made.

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 397 · Sahih Muslim 1329

TraditionBilal calls to prayer from the roof of the Kaaba

The Sira books, such as Ibn Hisham's, tell that on the day of the conquest the Prophet ﷺ asked Bilal to climb onto the Kaaba and give the call to prayer. Bilal, a former slave of Abyssinian origin once tortured in Mecca for his faith, raised the adhan above the city. Some Quraysh leaders were shocked, which makes the moment all the more striking: the despised man of yesterday was calling all of Mecca to prayer. This is an account from the historical tradition (Sira), not a hadith from the major collections.

Source: Wikipédia : Bilal ibn Rabah · Wikipédia (ar) : بلال بن رباح

TraditionThe key of the Kaaba and the Banu Shayba

Even before Islam, the guardianship of the Kaaba belonged to the family of Uthman ibn Talha, of the Banu Abd ad-Dar. On the day of the conquest, the Prophet ﷺ took the key to open the House, then gave it back to Uthman ibn Talha, saying it would stay in his family. Commentators link this episode to the verse ordering that trusts be returned to their rightful owners (sura 4, verse 58); this link comes from traditional reports whose chains are debated. To this day the key is kept by his descendants, known as the Banu Shayba.

Source: Wikipedia : Uthman ibn Talha · Coran 4:58 (quran.com)

Historical factThe Kaaba of Ibn al-Zubayr, then of al-Hajjaj

In 683, during the war between Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr and the Umayyads, a fire damaged the Kaaba. Ibn al-Zubayr rebuilt it, bringing the Hijr of Ishmael back inside and giving it two doors, following Aisha's hadith. In 692, al-Hajjaj's army besieged and bombarded Mecca, and Ibn al-Zubayr was killed. Shortly after, on the order of Caliph Abd al-Malik, al-Hajjaj restored the Kaaba to the Quraysh shape with a single raised door: essentially the form we see today.

Source: Wikipedia : Kaaba · Sahih Muslim 1333 (hadith d'Aïcha et Ibn az-Zubayr)

Historical factThe Qarmatians carry off the Black Stone

In 930, during the pilgrimage, the Qarmatians, a dissident Ismaili movement based in eastern Arabia, attacked Mecca. They killed many pilgrims, threw bodies into the well of Zamzam and tore out the Black Stone. They carried it to their capital in the al-Ahsa region. The Stone only returned to Mecca in 951-952, after a ransom, more than twenty years later, broken into several pieces.

Source: Wikipedia : Qarmatians · Britannica : Qarmatian

Historical factThe flood and the Ottoman Kaaba

Mecca lies at the bottom of a valley, and violent rains there have often caused floods. Under the Ottoman sultan Murad IV, a great flood in 1039 AH (around 1629-1630) brought down part of the Kaaba's walls. The House was then rebuilt with granite from the mountains of Mecca, following exactly the old plan. This Ottoman rebuilding is essentially the Kaaba seen today, restored again in 1996.

Source: Wikipedia : Kaaba · Wikipedia : Great Mosque of Mecca

Historical factThe kiswa, the black cloth of the Kaaba

According to Arab historians such as Ibn Hisham, a king of Yemen, As'ad Abu Karib (a Tubba'), was the first to cover the Kaaba with cloth, long before Islam; this is an old tradition. After the conquest, the Prophet ﷺ covered it with Yemeni cloth, and Caliph Umar sent for fabric from Egypt. For centuries Egypt made the kiswa and sent it each year in a great procession (the mahmal). Since 1927, and fully since 1962, it has been woven in Mecca: about 670 kg of silk dyed black, embroidered with verses in gold and silver thread, and replaced every year.

Source: Wikipedia : Kiswah · Wikipédia : Kiswa

Historical factA mosque enlarged over fourteen centuries

In the Prophet's ﷺ time there was only an open space around the Kaaba, surrounded by houses. Caliph Umar bought houses to enlarge it and built a low wall around 638; Uthman added the first covered galleries. The Abbasids, especially al-Mahdi from 779, made the largest enlargement of their age; then the Ottomans, with the architect Sinan around 1570, roofed the galleries with small domes. In the 20th and 21st centuries, the Saudi expansions (from 1955, then under Kings Fahd, Abdullah and Salman) made it one of the largest mosques in the world, able to hold more than a million worshippers.

Source: Wikipedia : Great Mosque of Mecca · Britannica : Great Mosque of Mecca

Authentic hadithA prayer worth a hundred thousand prayers

According to Jabir ibn Abdullah, the Prophet ﷺ said that a prayer in his mosque in Medina is better than a thousand prayers elsewhere, except the Sacred Mosque, and that a prayer in the Sacred Mosque is better than a hundred thousand prayers elsewhere. This hadith is recorded by Ibn Majah (no. 1406) and was graded authentic (sahih) by al-Albani and other scholars. The first part, about the thousand prayers, is also in al-Bukhari (no. 1190), from Abu Hurayra. That is why so many pilgrims try to pray there as much as they can.

Source: Sunan Ibn Majah 1406 · Sahih al-Bukhari 1190

Account with a weak chainAdam, first builder of the Kaaba?

Old accounts, reported notably by al-Azraqi and in some Quran commentaries, say that Adam was the first to build the House, or that angels built it before him, and that Noah's flood erased it until Abraham raised it again. These accounts have weak chains or stop at the generation after the companions; no authentic hadith confirms them. The Quran only speaks of Abraham and Ishmael raising the foundations of the House (sura 2, verse 127). Many scholars therefore remain cautious on this point.

Source: Tafsir Ibn Kathir 2:127 (quran.com) · Wikipedia : Kaaba

TraditionHagar and Ishmael buried by the Kaaba

According to the earliest Sira historians, such as Ibn Ishaq, Hagar and later Ishmael were buried in the Hijr, the semicircular space beside the Kaaba. No authentic hadith confirms this, and nothing is visible there: no grave and no marker. It is an old historical tradition, given here as such. It explains why the place bears Ishmael's name.

Source: Wikipedia : Hagar in Islam · Wikipedia : Hijr Ismail

Historical fact1979: the seizure of the Grand Mosque

On 20 November 1979, the first day of the year 1400 AH, an armed group led by Juhayman al-Otaybi seized the Sacred Mosque during the dawn prayer and announced that one of them was the Mahdi. Thousands of worshippers were trapped. After about two weeks of fighting, Saudi forces retook the mosque in early December; several hundred people died. The event deeply marked the Muslim world.

Source: Wikipedia : Grand Mosque seizure · Wikipédia : Prise de la Grande Mosquée de La Mecque

Al-Aqsa Mosque (Jerusalem)

QuranThe Night Journey

The Quran tells that Allah took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque in Mecca to “the farthest mosque”, al-Aqsa (sura 17, verse 1). Authentic hadiths tell that the Prophet ﷺ led the prophets in prayer there before the ascension. Al-Aqsa is one of the three mosques to which one may travel specially to pray (al-Bukhari).

Source: Coran 17:1 · al-Bukhari 1189

Historical factThe caliph Umar in Jerusalem

In 637-638, the patriarch Sophronius agreed to surrender Jerusalem on condition that the caliph came in person. Umar came, had the esplanade cleared of the rubbish covering it and had a simple wooden mosque built there. Later chronicles tell that he refused to pray in the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, so that Muslims would not take it over later.

Source: Wikipedia

Authentic hadithThe first qibla

After arriving in Medina, the Prophet ﷺ prayed for sixteen or seventeen months facing Jerusalem (Bayt al-Maqdis), while wishing to face the Kaaba. Then came verses 142 to 144 of Surat al-Baqara, which set the direction of prayer towards the Sacred Mosque in Mecca. According to al-Bukhari, people still praying towards Jerusalem heard the news in the middle of their prayer and turned at once towards the Kaaba. This is why Al-Aqsa is called "the first of the two qiblas". Authentic hadith (sahih).

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 40 · Wikipedia : Qibla

Authentic hadithThe second mosque on earth

The Companion Abu Dharr asked the Prophet ﷺ which mosque was built first on earth. He answered: the Sacred Mosque in Mecca. Then which? The Al-Aqsa mosque. And how long between the two? Forty years. This authentic hadith (al-Bukhari 3366) makes Al-Aqsa one of the oldest houses of prayer, long before the present buildings. Scholars explain that it refers to the first founding of the site, not to the building works of Solomon or of the Umayyads.

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 3366

Authentic hadithSolomon's three requests

According to a hadith reported by an-Nasa'i (no. 693), when the prophet Solomon (Sulayman) finished building Bayt al-Maqdis, he asked God for three things: judgement in harmony with God's judgement; a kingdom that no one after him would have; and that anyone who came to this mosque only to pray would leave free of sin, as on the day of his birth. The hadith says the first two were granted to him. It is graded authentic (sahih) by al-Albani and in the Darussalam edition.

Source: Sunan an-Nasa'i 693

Authentic hadithOne of the three mosques worth the journey

The Prophet ﷺ said that one sets out on a journey of devotion only to three mosques: the Sacred Mosque in Mecca, his mosque in Medina and the Al-Aqsa mosque. This authentic hadith is reported by al-Bukhari (1189) and Muslim. It gives Al-Aqsa the same rank for pious visits as the two great mosques of Arabia. For centuries, pilgrims from across the Muslim world extended their journey to Jerusalem for this reason.

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari 1189

Authentic hadithHow much is a prayer at Al-Aqsa worth?

The figures that circulate are not all reliable, and this must be said. The strongest report comes from Abu Dharr: one prayer in the Prophet's mosque is better than four prayers in Bayt al-Maqdis, "and what an excellent place of prayer it is". Al-Hakim graded it authentic and adh-Dhahabi agreed. Other figures, such as 500 or 50,000 prayers, come from reports graded weak, for example Ibn Majah 1413, graded da'if. Scholars agree on one point only: praying at Al-Aqsa carries great merit.

Source: Sunan Ibn Majah 1413 (faible) · HadithAnswers : hadith d'Abu Dharr

Historical factThe Dome of the Rock is not the Al-Aqsa mosque

Many people confuse two buildings. The Dome of the Rock, with its golden dome, was built in 691-692 by the Umayyad caliph Abd al-Malik over the Rock from which, in Muslim belief, the Prophet's ascension began. The grey-domed mosque at the south of the esplanade is called the al-Qibli mosque: it is the main prayer hall. For Muslims, "Al-Aqsa" really means the whole esplanade, with both buildings, its courtyards and fountains.

Source: Wikipedia : Dome of the Rock · Wikipedia : Al-Aqsa Mosque

Historical factThe builder's erased name

A long inscription in Kufic script runs inside the Dome of the Rock and gives the year 72 AH (691-692). Yet the name of its builder, the Umayyad caliph Abd al-Malik, was later erased. In its place, the name of the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun, who reigned more than a century later, was written. But whoever made the change forgot to alter the date, which still gives away the real builder.

Source: Wikipedia : Dome of the Rock

Historical factDestroyed by earthquakes, rebuilt again

The al-Qibli mosque was built by the Umayyads, Abd al-Malik or his son al-Walid I, in the early 8th century; papyri from Egypt even mention the workers sent to build it. An earthquake ruined it in 746. The Abbasid caliph al-Mansur rebuilt it in 758, melting down the gold and silver plates that covered its doors. Another earthquake struck in 1033; the Fatimid caliph az-Zahir rebuilt it smaller, and the present plan dates from that time.

Source: Wikipedia : Al-Aqsa Mosque

Historical factIn the time of the Crusaders

In 1099 the Crusaders took Jerusalem and the mosque ceased to be a place of Muslim prayer. The building served as a royal palace, and even as a stable. From 1119 it became the headquarters of an order of knight-monks, which took its very name from the place: the Templars. The Dome of the Rock was turned into a church. This lasted 88 years.

Source: Wikipedia : Al-Aqsa Mosque · Wikipedia : Knights Templar

Historical factThe minbar promised by Nur ad-Din

In 1168-1169, Sultan Nur ad-Din had a carved wooden minbar made in Aleppo, a masterpiece of woodwork, promising to install it in Al-Aqsa on the day Jerusalem was retaken. He died in 1174 without seeing that day. His successor Salah ad-Din (Saladin) retook the city in 1187 after a siege and restored the mosque to prayer. He then had the minbar brought from Aleppo to Al-Aqsa, fulfilling Nur ad-Din's promise. It remained there for nearly eight centuries.

Source: Wikipedia : Minbar of Saladin · Wikipedia : Siege of Jerusalem (1187)

Historical factThe 1969 fire and the rebuilt minbar

On 21 August 1969, an Australian visitor, Denis Michael Rohan, set fire to the al-Qibli mosque. Nur ad-Din's minbar was destroyed and part of the dome and its decoration burned. The fire led to a summit of Muslim countries, from which the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation was born. Craftsmen and experts gathered in Jordan then rebuilt the minbar identically, piece by piece, using the old techniques. The new minbar was installed in its place in 2007.

Source: Wikipedia : Al-Aqsa Mosque · Wikipedia : Minbar of Saladin

Historical factThe greatest dome of its age

The Byzantine emperor Justinian had Hagia Sophia ("Divine Wisdom") built in barely five years; it was completed in 537. It was then the largest interior space in the world, covered by a huge dome that seems to float on a crown of windows. For nearly a thousand years it was the cathedral of Constantinople. The architects of the Blue Mosque and the Süleymaniye later measured themselves against it.

Source: Wikipedia : Hagia Sophia · UNESCO : Historic Areas of Istanbul

Tradition29 May 1453: Mehmed II enters Hagia Sophia

On 29 May 1453, the young Ottoman sultan Mehmed II took Constantinople. He went to Hagia Sophia and ordered it turned into a mosque at once; a scholar climbed the ambo and proclaimed the profession of faith. Chronicles report that the sultan struck with his sword a soldier who was prising up a marble slab, saying the buildings now belonged to him. On the first Friday, 1 June 1453, he attended prayer there. The details of these scenes come from chroniclers of the time and may be embellished.

Source: Wikipedia : Hagia Sophia

Historical factThe eight great roundels

Between 1847 and 1849, Sultan Abdülmecid had Hagia Sophia restored by the Swiss architects Gaspare and Giuseppe Fossati. Eight huge wooden discs, more than 7 metres across, were then hung, bearing in gold letters the names of Allah, the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, the first four caliphs and the grandsons al-Hasan and al-Husayn. They were designed by the calligrapher Kazasker Mustafa İzzet Efendi. The roundels stayed in place when the building became a museum, and then a mosque again.

Source: Wikipedia : Hagia Sophia

Historical factMuseum in 1934, mosque in 2020

In 1934, the government of the Turkish Republic led by Mustafa Kemal Atatürk decided to turn Hagia Sophia into a museum; it opened to visitors in 1935. On 10 July 2020, Turkey's Council of State annulled that decision, and the president ordered the building reopened for Muslim worship. The first Friday prayer took place there on 24 July 2020. The Christian mosaics remain in place; some are veiled during prayers, and visits are still possible outside prayer times.

Source: Wikipedia : Hagia Sophia

Historical factWhy "blue"?

Sultan Ahmed I had his mosque built between 1609 and 1617, facing Hagia Sophia; the architect was Sedefkâr Mehmed Ağa, a pupil of the great Sinan. Its real name is the Sultan Ahmed Mosque. Visitors call it the "Blue Mosque" because of the thousands of Iznik ceramic tiles and mainly blue paintings that cover the interior. The sultan, who died young, is buried in a mausoleum right next to it.

Source: Wikipedia : Blue Mosque, Istanbul · UNESCO : Historic Areas of Istanbul

Popular legendSix minarets by mistake?

A popular legend says the sultan asked for "golden minarets" (altın minareler in Turkish). The architect supposedly heard "six minarets" (altı minare), since the two words sound alike. At the time, only the Sacred Mosque in Mecca had that many minarets, and the sultan was criticised for his pride. According to the same legend, he then paid for a seventh minaret at the mosque in Mecca. Nothing proves this story: it is a folk tale, but the six minarets are very real.

Source: Wikipedia : Blue Mosque, Istanbul

Historical factTwo popes visit

On 30 November 2006, Pope Benedict XVI visited the Blue Mosque with the mufti of Istanbul; he paused there in silence, facing the mihrab. It was the second papal visit to a mosque, after John Paul II's visit to the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus in 2001. Pope Francis visited it in turn in November 2014. The mosque remains open to visitors of all faiths outside prayer times.

Source: Wikipedia : Blue Mosque, Istanbul

Historical factFrom temple to mosque

The site has been sacred for three thousand years: the Arameans worshipped the god Hadad here, then the Romans built a huge temple of Jupiter whose walls still enclose the mosque. Under Byzantium it became the Cathedral of Saint John the Baptist. After the Muslim conquest of 634, part of it was set aside for Muslim prayer, next to the Christians. Around 705, Caliph al-Walid I acquired the whole site, gave the Christians other churches in compensation, and built the great mosque, completed in 715.

Source: Wikipedia : Umayyad Mosque · UNESCO : Ancient City of Damascus

TraditionThe head of Yahya (John the Baptist)

In the prayer hall stands a small venerated shrine: according to tradition, it holds the head of the prophet Yahya, whom Christians call John the Baptist. This tradition goes back to the 6th century, before Islam, and is shared by Christians and Muslims. It is said that a cave containing this reliquary was found during al-Walid's works. No historical proof confirms that it really is his head: it is a tradition, not an established fact. In 2001, Pope John Paul II paused there in prayer during his visit to the mosque.

Source: Wikipedia : Umayyad Mosque

TraditionThe Minaret of Jesus

In a long authentic hadith reported by Muslim (no. 2937), from an-Nawwas ibn Sam'an, the Prophet ﷺ announces that at the end of time Jesus son of Mary will descend by the white minaret, east of Damascus, with his hands on the wings of two angels. The hadith itself is sahih. However, identifying this "white minaret" with the south-eastern minaret of the Great Mosque, called the "Minaret of Jesus" (Isa), is a popular tradition. This minaret was destroyed and rebuilt several times, for example after a siege in 1245.

Source: Sahih Muslim 2937 · Wikipedia : Umayyad Mosque

Historical factAl-Walid's golden mosaics

Al-Walid wanted a mosque without equal: about 4,000 m² of gold-ground mosaics covered the courtyard and prayer hall, perhaps the largest area of gold mosaic in the world. They show trees, rivers and palaces, with no living beings. The 10th-century geographer al-Muqaddasi said that hardly a tree or a known town was missing from them. The historian Ibn Asakir relates that al-Walid obtained 200 craftsmen from the Byzantine emperor; historians debate this detail. The great "Barada panel", 34 m long, can still be seen today.

Source: Wikipedia : Umayyad Mosque

Historical factThe fire of 1893

In 1893, a worker repairing the roof was smoking his water pipe; an ember set the timber alight. The fire swept through the prayer hall and brought down the great central dome, called the "Eagle Dome". The Ottomans fully restored the mosque, and the new dome was built of stone instead of wood. It was not the first fire: the mosque had already burned in 1069, and it was badly damaged during Timur's invasion in 1401.

Source: Wikipedia : Umayyad Mosque

TraditionThe shrine of al-Husayn

In the eastern corner of the mosque, two rooms recall al-Husayn, grandson of the Prophet ﷺ, killed at Karbala in 680. According to tradition, his head was brought to Damascus, to the palace of Caliph Yazid, and a shrine marks the place where it is said to have been laid. Where the head truly rests is debated: sources name Damascus, Karbala, Cairo and other cities. The shrine draws many visitors today, both Sunni and Shia.

Source: Wikipedia : Umayyad Mosque

Historical factSinan, Suleiman's architect

Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent entrusted his great mosque to Mimar Sinan, the empire's architect, who built it between 1550 and 1557 on Istanbul's third hill. Around it Sinan built a whole charitable quarter: schools, a hospital, a kitchen for the poor, baths. The sultan rests in a mausoleum behind the mosque. Sinan himself, who lived nearly a hundred years and built hundreds of buildings, is buried in a small tomb just outside the complex, near the house where he lived.

Source: Wikipedia : Süleymaniye Mosque · Wikipedia : Mimar Sinan

TraditionFour minarets, ten balconies

The mosque has four minarets at the corners of the courtyard, carrying ten balconies in all. According to a traditional explanation, the four minarets recall that Suleiman was the fourth sultan to reign in Istanbul, and the ten balconies that he was the tenth Ottoman sultan. Among the Ottomans, only a sultan could give a mosque more than two minarets; princes and princesses had two, others only one. The number of balconies is a fact; its symbolic meaning is a tradition.

The first Friday prayer was held there in June 972, during Ramadan. In 989, the mosque's authorities hired 35 scholars to teach there, with lodging and pay. This was the start of a school that has never stopped for more than a thousand years. Students sat in circles around a master leaning against a pillar. They were grouped by country of origin in "riwaqs": the riwaq of the Maghrebis, of the Syrians, of the Turks… In 1735 a riwaq was even added for blind students. Historical factSaladin closes the pulpit, the Mamluks reopen it

Al-Azhar had been founded by the Fatimids, who followed Ismaili Shia doctrine. When Saladin ended their rule in 1171, he removed the mosque's role as a Friday mosque: his judge banned the Friday prayer there and students lost their stipends. The mosque was neglected for nearly a century. The Mamluk sultans, beginning with Baybars around 1266, restored the Friday prayer and enlarged it. Al-Azhar then became the great centre of Sunni learning it still is.

In 1798, Bonaparte occupied Egypt and tried to win over the scholars of al-Azhar: he set up a council of nine sheikhs to run Cairo and asked them, in vain, for a fatwa in his favour. On 21 October 1798, a revolt against the French troops started from al-Azhar; the army crushed it and horsemen entered the mosque. In 1800, General Kléber was killed by Sulayman al-Halabi, a student from Aleppo who lodged at al-Azhar. The French then closed the mosque, which reopened only after their departure in 1801.

Popular legendWhy “the olive tree”?

The name Zitouna means “the olive tree”. A legend tells that an olive tree grew on the spot of the first prayer place. The historian Ibn Abi Dinar (17th century) gives another explanation: the memory of a Christian martyr, Saint Olive. No one knows which is true.

Historical factThe scholar leaning against his column

At the Zitouna, every teacher taught leaning against a column. The traveller Ibn Battuta, passing through Tunis in the 14th century, tells that a scholar, Abu Ali Umar al-Hawwari, leaned every Friday against a column of the mosque to answer people's questions, up to forty of them, then left. For more than a thousand years the mosque trained judges and scholars; according to tradition, Ibn Khaldun studied there.

Source: ويكيبيديا

Historical factIbn Arafa and Ibn Khaldun, two rivals

In the 14th century, the imam of the Zitouna was Ibn Arafa (1316-1401), the greatest Maliki scholar of his day in Tunis. He knew well another son of Tunis, Ibn Khaldun, later the author of the Muqaddima. The two men did not get along: Ibn Arafa blamed Ibn Khaldun for being too close to the rulers, and Ibn Khaldun accused him of jealousy. Today everyone knows Ibn Khaldun, but in their own time it was Ibn Arafa who led the great mosque.

Source: Wikipedia · Webdo

Historical factThe Spanish soldiers and the lost books

In the 16th century Tunis was taken by the Spanish: by the troops of Charles V in 1535, and again in 1573. Soldiers entered the Zitouna, and the city's libraries, including the mosque's, were looted. Many manuscripts were carried off to Europe as booty; some are found today in libraries in Spain, Italy and Germany. Yet, according to Tunisian historians, prayer never stopped in the mosque.

Historical factThe students' strike of 1910

In April 1910, under the French protectorate, more than 800 Zitouna students went on strike. They asked for more modern teaching, better living conditions and shorter studies. They demonstrated before the government palace and occupied the great mosque at prayer time. After about ten days the authorities gave in: the arrested students were freed and a reform was begun. It was one of the first movements of its kind in modern Tunisia, and other strikes followed until independence.

Source: Webdo · OpenEdition (IREMAM)

Historical factTahar Ben Achour, the reforming rector

Mohamed Tahar Ben Achour (1879-1973) entered the Zitouna at 13 and taught there all his life. In 1932 he became its rector, but scholars attached to the old methods pushed him to resign the very next year. Back in 1945, he modernised the teaching, as he had proposed in a book devoted to reforming the Zitouna. He also wrote a great commentary on the Quran in thirty volumes, at-Tahrir wa at-Tanwir, on which he worked for nearly forty years.

For centuries the people of Tunis have gathered at the Zitouna for the feast of the Prophet's birth ﷺ. First the prayer book Dala'il al-Khayrat is read, then the imam tells the story of the Prophet's birth ﷺ, and the whole gathering sings the Hamziya, a long poem of praise. In the mid-19th century the grand imam and mufti Brahim Riahi had this feast celebrated officially, in the ruler's presence. Many Tunisians believe that the feast at the Zitouna protects the country all year long: this is a popular belief.

Popular legendOkba and the beasts of the forest

In 670, the spot chosen to found Kairouan was a thicket full of snakes and wild beasts. Legend says that Okba ibn Nafi called out: “We are the companions of the Messenger of Allah, go away!”, and that the lions, wolves and snakes left carrying their young. He is then said to have prayed for the town to be filled with knowledge, before laying out the great mosque. Historians see it as a founding legend, reported by Ibn Idhari.

Unfounded belief“Seven visits to Kairouan equal one pilgrimage”?

People often say that seven visits to Kairouan equal one pilgrimage to Mecca. This popular belief has no religious basis: no authentic hadith or recognised scholar supports it, and the pilgrimage is a pillar of Islam that belongs to Mecca alone. Historians link it to accounts praising the town that were invented in the early centuries.

Popular legendThe columns that cannot be counted

A legend says that you cannot count the columns of the prayer hall without going blind, and that two very close columns were a test: only those who could pass between them would go to Paradise. These are legends. What is true: its carved wooden minbar from the 9th century is the oldest minbar in the world still in its place.

Historical factSahnun, the judge “slain without a knife”

Imam Sahnun (c. 776-854) taught in Kairouan; he put in order the Mudawwana, the great book of Maliki law later studied across the Maghreb and al-Andalus. Around 848 the Aghlabid emir appointed him judge (qadi). On accepting, he told his daughter Khadija: “Today your father has been slain without a knife,” so much did the duty of judging frighten him. He was gentle with litigants but strict with the emir's men, and refused to overlook their illegal dealings. It is said he accepted gifts from no one.

Source: Wikipedia · Midad

Historical factThe Blue Quran and the Nurse's Quran

The Great Mosque kept one of the richest parchment libraries in the Muslim world; its oldest manuscripts date from the 9th century. It held pages of the famous Blue Quran, written in gold letters on parchment dyed indigo blue. It also kept the “Nurse's Quran”, copied around 1020 by Ali ibn Ahmad al-Warraq for the nurse of the Zirid prince al-Mu'izz. These treasures are now in the Raqqada museum near Kairouan and in the Bardo museum.

Around the mihrab shine about 160 lustre-glazed ceramic tiles, set in the 9th century under the emir Abu Ibrahim Ahmad. They closely resemble the ceramics of Abbasid Iraq. For a long time people wondered whether they were made in Baghdad, or in Kairouan by a craftsman from Baghdad. Recent research leans towards manufacture in Iraq, probably at Samarra, followed by a long journey to Kairouan.

In the middle of the courtyard, a few white marble steps lead up to a horizontal sundial. An inscription gives its maker's name, Ahmad ibn Qasim ibn Ammar al-Susi, and the date 1258 AH (1842-1843). It was used to set the times of the five prayers. Another small vertical sundial is fixed on the eastern portico. Beneath the courtyard, a great vaulted cistern collects rainwater, once drawn through well-heads carved from old column bases.

Historical factThe maqsura of Prince al-Mu'izz

Near the minbar stands a screen of carved cedar wood, the maqsura, where the prince prayed in shelter. It was made around 1022 for the Zirid prince al-Mu'izz ibn Badis; a Kufic inscription gives his name and that of his vizier. It is the oldest maqsura still in place in the Islamic world. The same prince also gave the mosque a great bronze lantern more than a metre high.

Source: Wikipédia · Wikipedia

Great Mosque of Sousse

Historical factA fortress-mosque facing the sea

The Great Mosque of Sousse was ordered in 851 by the Aghlabid emir Abu al-Abbas Muhammad. It has battlements and corner towers, like the nearby ribat, because the town feared attacks from the sea. It has no minaret: the muezzin called from a small domed pavilion at one corner of the courtyard.

Source: Wikipedia

Historical factA freed slave runs the building site

In 851 the Aghlabid emir Abu al-Abbas Muhammad ordered the Great Mosque of Sousse. He entrusted the work to Mudam, a former slave he had freed and who had become his trusted man. Sousse was growing fast, and the small mosque of the nearby ribat was no longer enough for the people. Later the prayer hall was extended southwards.

Source: Wikipedia · Wikipédia

Historical factA dome before the mihrab, as in Damascus

Before the original mihrab, a dome marks the place where the imam stands, as in the Great Mosque of Kairouan. Such domes were rare at the time: only a few older ones are known, at al-Aqsa Mosque in Jerusalem and the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus. In the 11th century the Zirids added the small domed tower from which the muezzin gave the call.

Source: Wikipedia

Great Mosque of Mahdia

Historical factWon from the sea, without a minaret

The Fatimid caliph Ubayd Allah al-Mahdi had the mosque built in 916, on a platform won from the sea according to the geographer al-Bakri. It had no minaret, and its monumental entrance imitates Roman triumphal arches. In the 11th century the sea swept away the qibla wall; in 1550 the troops of Charles V largely demolished it. It was rebuilt in 1961-1965: only the gateway and the portico are original.

Source: Wikipedia

Historical fact“The Man on the Donkey” at the gates of Mahdia

In 944 a Berber Ibadi leader, Abu Yazid, rose against the Fatimid caliphs. He was called “the Man on the Donkey” because he rode a plain donkey. On 9 January 945 he reached Mahdia and besieged it for eight months; his troops even reached the walls of the palace city. Mahdia held out. Defeated, Abu Yazid was captured and died of his wounds in August 947.

Source: Wikipedia

Popular legend“The Island of the Caliphs”

Chroniclers tell several stories about the founding of Mahdia by the caliph Ubayd Allah al-Mahdi. In one, he meets a priest on the peninsula who tells him the place is called “the Island of the Caliphs”. Other accounts say soothsayers helped him choose the site, or credit him with predictions about the city's future. Historians see these as legends written to glorify the city and its founder, as for many cities of the East.

Source: IJAHCT (2018)

Historical factMarble taken to Pisa?

In 1087 a fleet from Pisa and Genoa attacked and sacked Mahdia, then the Zirid capital. The mosque was probably damaged, since its prayer hall was afterwards repaired and altered. Some historians think the church of San Sisto in Pisa may have been built with marble torn from the mosque; this is not proven. Much later, around 1798, the minister Yusuf Sahib at-Taba' had the mosque restored.

Source: Wikipedia

Historical factA model for the mosques of Cairo

Mahdia was the first capital of the Fatimids, before they left for Egypt and founded Cairo. Their mosque in Mahdia became a model: its great projecting entrance gate is found again in Fatimid mosques in Cairo, such as the al-Hakim Mosque, begun in 990. The two square towers at the corners of the Mahdia façade were long taken for minaret bases; they were in fact rainwater tanks for ablutions.

Source: Wikipedia

Great Mosque of Sfax

Historical factA mosque wanted by a judge

The Great Mosque of Sfax was founded around 849 at the initiative of the judge (qadi) Ali ibn Aslam al-Jabanyani, a pupil of Imam Sahnun of Kairouan; its minaret recalls that of Kairouan. In 1881, during the siege of Sfax by the French fleet, the minaret was badly damaged by the bombardment.

Source: Wikipedia

Historical factThe mosque turned into barracks

In July 1881 Sfax resisted the arrival of French troops and was bombarded by the fleet; the minaret was hit. After the city was taken, French soldiers settled in the Great Mosque and made it a barracks: they washed their clothes and tied their horses there. During the Second World War, in 1942, two more bombs fell on the mosque. It was extensively repaired after independence.

Source: Wikipedia

Historical factTwo Byzantine peacocks on the façade

The mosque's eastern façade bears two Kufic inscriptions dated 988 and 1085, in Zirid times. Above one window is a reused Byzantine marble panel showing two peacocks, now damaged, with a half-erased Greek inscription. No one really knows why it was put there: simple reuse, or the tolerance of the time.

Source: Wikipedia · Wikipédia

Historical factThe craftsmen of 1758

In the 10th century the mosque had been cut down to half its size. In 1758 and 1774, under the Husainid beys, it was enlarged again to the west, giving it its present shape. The craftsmen's names are known: the master mason Haj Sa'id al-Qatti, Tahar al-Manif who made the new mihrab, and the poet Ali al-Ghurab, author of the inscription above it. The old Zirid mihrab, walled up at that time, has since been reopened.

Source: Wikipedia

Mosque of the Three Doors

Historical factAn Andalusian signs his mosque

On the façade, a carved inscription says that the mosque was built by order of Muhammad ibn Khayrun al-Ma'afiri al-Andalusi, a man from al-Andalus, in 866. According to the historian Mourad Rammah, it is the oldest decorated façade in Islamic architecture. The minaret was added around 1440, under the Hafsids.

Source: Wikipedia

TraditionThe master of Quran recitation

Biographers mention a Muhammad ibn Khayrun al-Ma'afiri, from al-Andalus, who was the master of Quran reciters in Kairouan. According to them, he brought to Ifriqiya the reading of the Quran of Nafi', in the transmission of Warsh, the one recited in Tunisia today; before him the reading of Hamza prevailed. He was also a merchant and built a mosque in Kairouan that bears his name. He is usually identified with the founder of the Mosque of the Three Doors, but this is not certain. Accounts of his death in 918 also differ: in Sousse according to some, killed on the order of the Fatimid governor of Kairouan according to others.

Source: Areq (encyclopédie) · Tarajm

Historical factA prayer carved in stone

The façade bears three bands of Kufic writing. The upper two contain verses of the Quran (sura 33, verses 71-72, and a passage of sura 30). The lower band says that Muhammad son of Khayrun ordered this building to draw closer to God, hoping for His pardon and mercy. In 1440, when the mosque was restored and the minaret added, a new inscription was slipped into this band, somewhat mixing up the old ones.

Source: Wikipedia

Youssef Dey Mosque

Historical factThe first Ottoman mosque in Tunis

Built from 1612 by Youssef Dey, it has the first octagonal minaret in Tunis, in the Ottoman manner, and a stone minbar, whereas the city's Maliki mosques had wooden minbars. Its founder is buried there.

Source: Wikipedia

Historical factThe dey from Tripoli and his souks

Youssef Dey was born around 1560 in Tripoli, the son of a Turkish soldier. He joined the Tunis militia and won the trust of Othman Dey, who gave him his daughter in marriage and had him chosen as his successor. He ruled from 1610 until his death in 1637. Around his mosque he had Turkish souks built: the Souk El Trouk (of the Turks), the Souk El Berka and the slipper-makers' Souk El Bchamkiya. He was buried in the mausoleum he had built next to it.

Source: Wikipédia

Historical factAn Andalusian architect, an annex of the Zitouna

The mosque's architect is known as Ibn Ghalib al-Andalusi: a name that recalls the Andalusians who settled in Tunis after being expelled from Spain, in Youssef Dey's own time. Restored by Ali Bey at the end of the 19th century, the mosque became by decree, in 1926, an annex of the Zitouna university, where lessons were given.

Source: Wikipedia

Hammouda Pacha Mosque

Historical factA mosque and its founder's tomb

Built in 1655 by Hammouda Pacha Bey, of the Muradid dynasty, it is the second Hanafi mosque in Tunis, with an octagonal minaret. In its courtyard stands the mausoleum of its founder, decorated with coloured marble.

Source: Wikipedia

Historical factAziza Othmana, the generous wife

Hammouda Pacha married a princess, a granddaughter of Othman Dey. The people called her “Aziza”, “the beloved”, for her kindness. At the end of her life she freed all her slaves and gave her lands as habous, a pious endowment, to free slaves, ransom prisoners and pay the trousseau of poor girls. She also founded a hospital in the medina: the Aziza Othmana hospital in Tunis still bears her name. She died in 1669.

Source: Wikipédia · Wikipédia

Historical factA tomb that inspired Bourguiba's

Hammouda Pacha died in 1666 at Dar El Bey, the palace he had built. He was buried in the mausoleum in his mosque's courtyard, in the corner opposite the minaret. This mausoleum, with its coloured marbles, served as a model for the mausoleum of President Habib Bourguiba in Monastir, inaugurated in 1963. The mosque still hosts weddings and circumcision celebrations today.

Source: Wikipédia · Wikipédia

Sidi Mahrez Mosque (Mohamed Bey Mosque)

TraditionThe “Sultan of the medina”

Sidi Mahrez ibn Khalaf (951-1022), a Maliki scholar, is the patron saint of Tunis. Tradition says he helped rebuild the city's walls and souks after the destruction of Abu Yazid's revolt; he is called “Soltane El Médina”. A legend adds that he obtained for the Jews, who had to spend the night outside the walls, the right to live in the city: a stick was thrown from the top of a minaret, and the Jewish quarter, the Hara, was built where it fell.

Source: Wikipédia

Historical factAn Istanbul mosque in Tunis

The mosque, built from 1692 to 1697 by Mohamed Bey El Mouradi, copies the model of the great imperial mosques of Istanbul, with its large dome surrounded by half-domes: it is the only one of its kind in Tunisia. Left unfinished because of political unrest, it has no minaret. Its white domes appear on the emblem of the city of Tunis.

Source: Wikipédia

Tradition“Yehrez Mahrez”: without him it would have been worse

Born in Ariana, Sidi Mahrez settled in Tunis around 1014, in a house in the Bab Souika quarter. When he died in 1022 he was buried in his own house, which became his zaouia, right opposite the mosque that bears his name. The people of Tunis still say “Yehrez Mahrez”, roughly “Mahrez protects”: without him things would have been worse. According to a Tunisian writer, the saying very probably dates from the time he gave the people courage again after Abu Yazid's destruction.

Source: Webdo · Wikipedia

Historical factDomes on a platform

The Sidi Mahrez mosque is built on a platform more than four metres above the surrounding streets. Its central dome is nearly 16 metres across and rises to 29 metres. Some of its ceramic tiles were imported from Iznik in Turkey and have a very distinctive tomato red. A major restoration began in 1984, together with that of the Bab Souika quarter.

Source: Wikipédia

Unfounded beliefVows at the zaouia

The zaouia of Sidi Mahrez, opposite the mosque, is one of the most visited shrines in Tunisia. According to popular custom, women come there to pray for a husband or for children. This is a popular belief: in Islam, Allah alone is invoked, and visiting a grave is for greeting the dead and praying for them.

Source: Wikipedia · Webdo

Zawiya of Sidi Sahbi (Mosque of the Barber)

TraditionThe companion who kept hairs from the Prophet's beard ﷺ

Abu Zam'a al-Balawi, a companion of the Prophet ﷺ, died fighting the Byzantines around 654 and was buried here, even before Kairouan was founded. According to tradition, he carried a few hairs from the Prophet's beard ﷺ. This is where the nickname “Mosque of the Barber” comes from: he was not a barber. “Sidi Sahbi” means “my lord the Companion”. Most of the present building dates from the 17th century.

Source: Tunisie Patrimoine · Wikipedia

Historical factTwo beys and tiles from Tunis

In the 14th century a first mausoleum was built over the tomb of Abu Zam'a al-Balawi. In 1629 Hammouda Pacha Bey, founder of the Hammouda Pacha mosque in Tunis, greatly enlarged it with a prayer hall and rooms for pilgrims. His successor Mohamed Bey rebuilt the mausoleum's dome between 1681 and 1685, then added a minaret and a madrasa. The walls are covered with painted tiles from Qallaline, the potters' quarter of Tunis, in blue, green and yellow, topped by carved stucco.

Source: Wikipedia

TraditionThe Mawlid and the little boys

Kairouan celebrates the feast of the Prophet's birth ﷺ (the Mawlid) with special fervour. In the past, after his circumcision, a little boy would cross the town with his family and visit the mausoleum of Sidi Sahbi. Even today, during the Mawlid, families and associations organise circumcisions there for poor or orphaned children, amid ululations, incense and henna. After the feast, Kairouan families come to the mausoleum to recite the Fatiha.

Source: La Presse (2025) · Vikidia

Great Mosque of Testour

Historical factThe Andalusians expelled from Spain

Testour was founded by Moriscos, Muslims expelled from Spain after King Philip III's decree of 1609. They built the Great Mosque around 1630, with a minaret that looks like the bell towers of their native Aragon.

Source: Wikipedia

Historical factThe clock that runs backwards

At the top of the minaret, a clock runs from right to left. No one really knows why. The most common explanation is nostalgia for al-Andalus, as if to turn back time; others speak of a direction turned towards Mecca or of protection against the evil eye. Stopped for nearly three centuries, it was started again in 2014.

Source: WMC, 23/04/2026

Historical factMohamed Tagharinu, the Andalusian founder

The Great Mosque of Testour was built before 1631 by Mohamed Tagharinu, a descendant of Andalusian families who had settled in Testour around 1609. Its 22.5-metre minaret combines a square tower and an octagonal tower covered in ceramics. In the 18th century a second courtyard and an ablution room were added. In the courtyard, a sundial bears the date 1174 AH (1761) and the name of its maker, Ahmed al-Harrar.

Source: Wikipédia · Wikipedia

TraditionThe stars of the minaret

The minaret is decorated with six-pointed stars. This is not a symbol foreign to Islam: it is the “Seal of Solomon”, found on many mosques, doors and zaouias in Tunisia. It is also said that Jewish Moriscos, who came from Spain with the Muslims and were well settled in Testour, may have helped build the minaret; this is possible, but there is no proof.

Source: Wikipédia

Malik ibn Anas Mosque (Carthage)

Historical factA mosque facing the ruins of Carthage

Completed in 2003, the mosque stands on the Odeon hill, next to the Roman theatre, facing the ruins of Carthage and the sea: thirteen centuries after the arrival of Islam, a great mosque on the site of the ancient Punic and Roman capital.

Source: Mosqpedia

Historical factA mosque that changed its name

When it opened in 2003, the Carthage mosque was called the El Abidine mosque, after the president of the time, Zine El Abidine Ben Ali. After the revolution, on 23 November 2012, President Moncef Marzouki, who had come for the Friday prayer, announced that it would henceforth be called the Malik ibn Anas mosque, after the great imam of Medina. In July 2025 the Minister of Religious Affairs launched its restoration.

Source: African Manager (2012) · La Presse (2025)

Historical factWhy Imam Malik?

Imam Malik ibn Anas (d. 795) taught in Medina. His students from Ifriqiya, and then Imam Sahnun of Kairouan, spread his school of law in the Maghreb: most Tunisians still follow the Maliki school. Naming the great mosque of Carthage after him recalls this link. The mosque, built of beige Kedhel stone quarried south of Tunis and of yellow and red Chemtou marble, holds about 1,700 worshippers.

Source: Mosqpedia · Wikipedia

Fadhloun Mosque and the Ibadi mosques of Djerba

Historical factA mosque that fed its neighbourhood

The Fadhloun mosque on Djerba probably dates from the 14th century. Originally Ibadi, it was part of a line of fortified mosques along the coast. It had its own Quranic school, grain mill and bakery, whose income paid its costs, and it supplied water from its cistern to the neighbouring houses.

Source: Patrimoine de Tunisie

TraditionAs many mosques as days in the year

A Djerba saying claims the island has as many mosques as there are days in the year. The old Ibadi mosques, like Fadhloun, are small and plain, built with local materials by and for the people of the hamlets, sometimes for only a dozen worshippers. They must be whitewashed with lime regularly, or the stone decays. As a rule they had no minaret, except to watch the coast.

Source: Les clés du Moyen-Orient (V. Prevost)

Historical factMosques as the island's ramparts

On Djerba, mosques also protected the people. Those on the coast formed the first line: from them the sea was watched and the alarm given. Fortified mosques, like Fadhloun, formed a second line of defence, while those inland, some dug underground, served as refuges and also hosted teaching. In 2023 UNESCO inscribed Djerba on the World Heritage List for this island settlement pattern, of which these mosques are a part.

Source: Patrimoine de Tunisie · UNESCO

Kasbah Mosque (Tunis)

TraditionThe white flag of the call to prayer

At prayer time, a white flag is raised at the top of the minaret. It is said to have been a signal to the other mosques: the Kasbah mosque, built around 1230-1235 by the first Hafsid ruler, stands at the highest point of the medina, near the palace, and the city's muezzins began the call when they saw it. The custom has returned in recent years.

Source: Wikipedia · Webdo

Historical factThe mosque of a prince who became king

In 1229 Abu Zakariya Yahya, governor of Tunis, declared his independence from the Almohads and founded the Hafsid dynasty, which reigned for more than three centuries. He had just begun a palace in the Kasbah, and he had this mosque built beside it, completed in 1233 by the architect Ali ibn Muhammad ibn Qasim. At first reserved for the palace, it later became a Friday mosque open to the whole city: the first built in Tunis after the Zitouna.

Source: Wikipedia

Historical factA minaret copied by the Zitouna

The Kasbah minaret, built of stone, follows the Almohad style of Morocco, like that of the Kasbah Mosque in Marrakesh. It inspired other minarets in Tunisia, including Testour's. Above all, when the Zitouna's minaret was rebuilt in 1894, it imitated the Kasbah's. The first call to prayer from the new Zitouna minaret was given on 26 Ramadan 1312, in the presence of Ali Bey.

Source: Wikipedia · Wikipédia

Historical factAn Ottoman-style stone minbar

After the Ottomans came to Tunis, the mosque was renovated in 1584 and its wooden minbar replaced by a stone one, in the Turkish manner. Above the mihrab, a muqarnas dome, with carved honeycomb-like cells, is almost unique in Ifriqiya.

Source: Wikipedia

Great Mosque of Monastir

Historical factScholars on retreat by the sea

The Great Mosque of Monastir, with its vaulted 9th-century hall, stands next to the ribat, a fortress where the coast was guarded and where people prayed. The scholars of Kairouan came there on retreat, between prayer, study and watching over the sea.

Source: Chapoutot-Remadi, Afkar/Idées (IEMed)

Historical factImam al-Mazari rests in Monastir

Imam al-Mazari (1061-1141), one of the greatest Maliki scholars, lived in Mahdia, where he died. Yet he was buried in Monastir, the town of the ribat and the Great Mosque, where his mausoleum is still visited. A student of al-Lakhmi of Sfax, he wrote al-Mu'lim, a commentary on Imam Muslim's collection of hadiths. Malikis simply call him “the Imam”.

Source: Wikipedia
